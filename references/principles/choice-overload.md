# Choice Overload

## Principle and evidence

A large or hard-to-compare set can make a decision feel effortful. Improve the decision process by exposing meaningful differences and helping people narrow the set.

Choice overload is context dependent. The catalog describes a psychological concept, not a universal maximum number of options. Expertise, preference clarity, option quality, and comparison difficulty affect whether a broad selection helps or hinders.

## Observable triggers

- People repeatedly switch options without reaching a decision.
- Labels offer little distinction, or comparison requires opening many separate screens.
- A catalog or settings page is large, but the relevant subset depends on user intent.

## Implementation actions

1. Identify the dimensions people actually use to decide. Replace vague labels with attributes, consequences, and relevant constraints.
2. Offer reversible filters, search, grouping, or a shortlist. Show active filters, result count, and a clear reset so narrowing does not become a hidden restriction.
3. Align comparable attributes in the same order. Mark unavailable or genuinely incompatible choices with reasons; disclose tradeoffs near selection.
4. If recommending an option, explain the basis and retain viable alternatives. Use a default only when its effect is understood, reversible, and appropriate to the audience.
5. Preserve an expert path such as a searchable full list or command palette. Test both people exploring options and people arriving with a known target.

## Example

A video export menu offers many presets with similar names. Group them by destination, then show resolution, format, estimated size, and compatibility in a comparison panel. Keep a custom preset route for editors who know their requirements. Remember a deliberate selection without silently changing it after a background capability check.

## Counterexample

Removing most export formats creates a smaller menu but prevents users from meeting a client's delivery specification. The apparent reduction in choice moves the work into unsupported external conversion.

## Limits and conflicts

Breadth may be valuable for discovery and specialist work. Progressive disclosure introduces its own navigation and memory costs. Compare end-to-end completion and decision confidence rather than counting visible options. Neutral comparison should not disguise a commercially preferred choice as an objectively best choice.

## Acceptance checks

- Users can explain the relevant difference between plausible alternatives.
- Filtering, resetting, and reaching the full supported set work through relevant input modes.
- A known-target user can reach their option without exploring every group.
- Recommendation rationale and consequential defaults are visible before commitment.
