# Peak–End Rule

## Principle and evidence

Memorable intense moments and endings can influence retrospective evaluation. Improve stressful transitions and truthful closure while also examining the whole experience.

The catalog summarizes a retrospective judgment effect from psychological research. It does not establish that users ignore duration or every ordinary moment, nor that a pleasant ending can compensate for harm. Treat expected changes in remembered satisfaction as a contextual hypothesis.

## Observable triggers

- A successful task ends without confirmation or next steps.
- One stressful error dominates users' recollection.
- A celebratory ending masks unresolved work or partial failure.

## Implementation actions

1. Map consequential and emotionally intense moments such as payment, publication, loss, cancellation, or recovery. Observe them alongside routine completion, not only through retrospective ratings.
2. Prevent avoidable anxiety through clear scope, predictable feedback, and preserved work. Offer practical recovery when things go wrong rather than a cheerful message alone.
3. Provide factual closure: confirmed outcome, affected items, destination or reference, unresolved issues, and an appropriate next action. State partial success explicitly.
4. Use celebration or supportive copy proportionally to the user's goal and context. Respect reduced motion, attention, cultural differences, and professional or sensitive settings.
5. Evaluate both the immediate outcome and remembered experience. Track task success, errors, lost work, recovery, and satisfaction separately; investigate conflicts instead of optimizing the final impression alone.

## Example

After publishing a presentation, the completion panel names the destination and published version, shows which assets failed, and offers a retry for those assets. It confirms what the audience can currently see. A short success treatment is appropriate only after the promised result is actually confirmed.

## Counterexample

A purchase flow ends in confetti after a confusing surcharge disclosure. The team reports improved final-screen ratings while ignoring customers who misunderstood the charge or abandoned before reaching that screen.

## Limits and conflicts

Long-term trust, repeat use, and domain consequences can outweigh a momentary impression. Ending an experience can mean many things: a completed action, a session, cancellation, or failure. Each needs appropriate closure. Artificially extending a task or hiding a negative outcome to create a pleasant ending undermines control and honest feedback.

## Acceptance checks

- Completion accurately describes confirmed, partial, pending, and failed outcomes.
- Stressful points have proportionate prevention and recovery support.
- Evaluation includes people who fail or abandon before the ending.
- Appearance or remembered satisfaction does not override unresolved task defects.
